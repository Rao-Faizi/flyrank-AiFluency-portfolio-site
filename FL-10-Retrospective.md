# Capstone Retrospective (FL-10)
**By: Muhammad Faizan**

## What I Set Out To Do
In Week 1, I set out to transition from someone who was merely "interested in AI" to an engineer who could systematically build, harden, and deploy AI-driven business automation solutions. My goal for this capstone was to create a portfolio that wasn't just a collection of generic side projects, but a professional asset that clearly communicated my specific value proposition: I automate manual workflows to save businesses time and money. I wanted a site that proved my competency before I even spoke a word in an interview.

## What Changed
Everything about my approach to software engineering shifted. At the start of the track, my instinct was to jump straight into code and focus heavily on technical implementation details. 

Through the FlyRank process—especially surviving the design critique (FL-17) and actively trying to break my own site (FL-18)—I learned that code is secondary to the business outcome. I shifted my portfolio's focus from "Here are the languages I know" to the STAR method (Problem, Solution, Impact). I learned the crucial skill of accepting raw design feedback without defending my work, leading me to drastically strip down my UI to focus on readability, high-contrast accessibility, and a clear call-to-action.

Most importantly, my relationship with AI evolved. Instead of treating AI as an oracle that writes code for me, I learned to treat it as a pair-programming partner. I stepped into the role of Lead Engineer—directing the architecture, reviewing the code, and owning the QA process.

## What I'd Build Next
My next step is to transition this static portfolio into a dynamic demonstration of my skills. I plan to build a live, interactive Chatbot interface embedded directly into the site, allowing visitors to ask questions about my resume and experience via an RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) pipeline. Furthermore, I want to take the "Known Limitations" from my FL-18 QA Audit—specifically the lack of backend spam prevention—and implement a robust, serverless validation layer using a microservice architecture. 

## The Three Most Transferable Things I Learned
1. **The Diligence of Breaking Things:** Knowing exactly where your software fails (and documenting those limitations honestly) is a superpower. Employers trust engineers who understand their own blind spots.
2. **AI as a Collaborative Tool, Not a Crutch:** I learned how to use agentic AI (like Antigravity) to accelerate my output by 10x, while still maintaining complete technical ownership of the architecture and final deliverables.
3. **Designing for the User's "Job to be Done":** I stopped building things for myself and started building for the reviewer. Structuring my work around business impact (e.g., "reduced manual drafting by 90%") rather than just listing tech stacks completely changed how my skills are perceived.
